**Pioneers**

Pioneering climbers such as Siegfried Herford, inventor of the Girdle Traverse, and Colin Kirkus, who was an unmatched climber until Joe Brown’s emergence, were both sadly claimed by war. Herford, a private in the Fusiliers, was killed by a grenade blast in January 1916, whilst Kirkus, a navigator in the RAF, was killed in September 1942.

There are some fantastic articles, including Jeff Connor’s piece for[ UKC on Herford](https://www.ukclimbing.com/articles/features/remembering_the_fallen-11424) and the late John Appleby’s[ Footless Crow blog on Colin Kirkus](https://footlesscrow.blogspot.com/2021/04/colin-kirkus-pathfinder.html). In this piece, we’re looking to explore how the military has shaped climbing more generally.

**Gear**

Speaking to founder and patron John Innerdale whilst looking around the Pillar 200 Exhibition at the Beacon, he remembered how he sourced his climbing equipment:

_“We used to go down to the war department stores and purchase the WD oval karabiners and boots.”_

Looking through the archives, you can see that the equipment used post-war, up until the late 1950s, all had a similar style and design. Karabiners were used throughout WWII with numerous applications. In fact, karabiners were invented during the 1600s to allow rifles to be carried on horseback by the military.

Post-war, there was a surplus of hard-wearing boots, clothing, karabiners, ropes and other pieces of equipment available to the general public. Not only was this equipment ubiquitous, but there also wasn’t really anything else available at the time that was suitable for the adventurous sport of climbing. With rationing continuing well into the 1950s, this was the only realistic way to purchase climbing equipment.

The effect of this ubiquitous military-turned-climbing gear was no doubt a driving force in the[ aforementioned](https://www.mountain-heritage.org/blog/launch-of-pillar-200-at-the-beacon/) democratisation of climbing. With readily available kit, climbing became more accessible to the working class of the period, and we started to see a boom in talented climbers who otherwise may not have been able to enter the somewhat elitist climbing circles of the pre-war period.

**The RAF Mountain Rescue**

On 6 July 1943, the[ RAF Mountain Rescue](https://rafmountainrescue.com/) was formed by Flight Lieutenant George Graham. Those in the military or familiar with Mountain Rescue may be aware of the size and importance of RAF Mountain Rescue, but those who have only seen their teams training at Cairngorm in the winter may not know its origins.

The RAF created the Mountain Rescue Association (RAF MRS) to locate missing pilots and crew members when planes were shot down or crashed during the war. With high ground often obscured by poor weather, flying was particularly hazardous for pilots and often led to difficult rescues for the nearest medical officers.

If you’ve ever been on those high tops in the Lakes, you’ll see RAF pilots practising their mountainous flying and may even have been lucky enough to see into the cockpit. With advanced technology and training, these crashes are less frequent, but during the Second World War, this was a hazard that had never really been encountered before. The medical officers were not equipped to search the high mountains for crew, and by 1943, the wheels were set in motion to create a formal association to rescue stranded pilots and crew.

The RAF MRS has gone on to be instrumental in building the foundations of Mountain Rescue techniques used by our volunteer Mountain Rescue associations today.

**Adventurous Training**

With AT (adventurous training) only becoming an official part of military training in the 1960s, the large proportion of military personnel in climbing history is mostly reflected by the proportion of the population serving in the armed forces during the World Wars, as well as the attitudes of individuals. Our own founder and patron, Chris Bonington, moved to the Army Outward Bound School after serving in the Tank Regiment, where he shared his climbing knowledge in North Wales as a mountaineering instructor.

In 1999, the Joint Service Mountain Training Centre was established. Today, adventurous training is an established part of the Services, with many military personnel being active climbers and mountaineers.

AT is not only used for resilience-building and team bonding. With conflicts being fought all over the world, there are plenty of examples of climbing and mountaineering skills being deployed. The[ Norwegian Heavy Water Sabotage Mission](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-sneak-attack-by-norways-skiing-soldiers-deprived-nazis-atomic-bomb-180968278/) during the Second World War saw Norwegian ski mountaineers parachuting onto the Hardangervidda plateau and sabotaging Nazi atomic bomb supplies - heavy water at the Rjukan Waterfall power station (pictured).

Having the skills that come with time in the mountains, both physical and mental, is an important part of a military member’s toolbox.

**More to come?**

Whilst this blog offers some leads for further exploration, we want to take this opportunity to remember the brave people who have defended this country and allowed us the freedom to travel the UK and beyond. Without them, we would not have access to our favourite crags and Alpine objectives.

We hope to explore Mountains and the Military further. With trustees and volunteers who have a deep connection to the military and mountaineering, we hope to open this window even wider.
